Deliver a small feature, learn, then adjust

Start with the explanation. Read the RFQ example next. Then spend 15 minutes on the exercise without opening its answer. Revisit the interview questions tomorrow. This lesson prepares you to explain delivery decisions in a senior engineering interview; reading it does not establish team delivery experience.

A simple starting point

Imagine making a sandwich for someone. Ask what they need before preparing five different meals. Give them something useful, check their response, and adjust. Software needs the same feedback, but also tests, security and operational care. A small release must still be safe.

Agile development uses short feedback cycles to adapt work. Working software and collaboration help a team learn whether it is solving the right problem. Documentation and plans still matter. Keep them useful and revise them when evidence changes. See the Agile principles.

Scrum gives the team a regular cycle

A Sprint lasts at most one month. The Product Owner orders the Product Backlog. Developers plan and deliver the work. The Scrum Master helps the team use Scrum effectively. A Sprint Goal gives the team a shared outcome. The selected work and delivery plan form the Sprint Backlog.

  • Planning: agree on the goal, select feasible work and plan its delivery.
  • Daily Scrum: Developers inspect progress and adapt their plan in 15 minutes.
  • Review: inspect the result with stakeholders and discuss what to change next.
  • Retrospective: inspect how the team worked and select improvements.

Refinement continuously clarifies and splits backlog items; it is not a separate required Scrum event. Scope can be renegotiated as the team learns, while protecting the Sprint Goal. Done means meeting the shared Definition of Done. Releases need not wait for the Review. See the Scrum Guide.

Example: show new RFQs to a trader

The following workflow is an illustrative team practice. It is not a requirement of Scrum. An RFQ is a request for a price quote.

  1. Discover the need. Ask the trader what decision the screen supports. Which instruments matter? What makes a quote stale? Who may see each request?
  2. Write the outcome. “An authorized trader can see a new RFQ for one instrument type.” Start with a read-only screen. Defer booking and additional instrument types.
  3. Refine the item. Agree on timestamp format, required fields, duplicate handling and upstream examples. Name dependencies and unresolved questions. Timebox a small investigation if the upstream contract is unknown.
  4. Define acceptance criteria. For this example, repeated upstream IDs produce one row. Missing prices display “unavailable,” not zero. Unauthorized users cannot retrieve the record. The chosen freshness threshold is visible and tested.
  5. Plan a vertical slice. Deliver ingestion, storage, API and UI for the one instrument type. Separate frontend-only and backend-only milestones can postpone useful feedback.
  6. Build and review. Use small pull requests. Show reviewers the contract, failure cases and tests. Resolve correctness and security issues before merging; distinguish optional style suggestions.
  7. Release and learn. Demonstrate with representative records. Observe freshness and errors. Record trader feedback, then reorder the next work.

Acceptance criteria describe this item's expected behavior. A Definition of Done applies common quality requirements across items. For this team, it could require reviewed code, passing tests, permission checks, usable error states and updated operational instructions. Passing happy-path acceptance criteria alone would not satisfy that definition.

Kanban makes waiting visible

Kanban uses an explicit workflow, controls work in progress and improves flow. Define where work starts and finishes. Track work in progress, completed-item throughput, unfinished-item age and completed-item cycle time. A service-level expectation forecasts how long work usually takes; it is not a guarantee. See the Kanban Guide.

Scrum can suit a team that benefits from a shared goal and regular inspection. Kanban can suit work with frequent arrivals and variable urgency. A Scrum team can also use Kanban flow controls. Neither approach removes the need to prioritize or manage incidents.

Estimates, interruptions and technical debt

Use estimates to expose uncertainty and discuss scope. Story points are optional. Do not compare individual performance or different teams using point totals. A forecast based on past work still needs capacity and dependency assumptions. Tell stakeholders which assumptions could change the date.

Suppose a production incident arrives during the RFQ work. Triage it, make displaced work visible and discuss scope with the Product Owner. Do not quietly add it to an unchanged promise. Protect quality when negotiating dates. If a dependency is late, offer a useful smaller slice or explain why no safe slice exists.

Technical debt belongs in delivery discussions. Describe its consequence: “This parser makes every new instrument require three coordinated edits.” Propose a bounded improvement, evidence of benefit and a review date. Avoid both indefinite cleanup projects and permanently postponing reliability work.

A useful retrospective action is testable: “For the next two weeks, rotate a reviewer each morning and measure review wait time.” “Communicate better” gives nobody a concrete action. Check whether faster review increases defects or reviewer overload.

Try one delivery decision

You have five working days. The upstream team can provide sample RFQs tomorrow, but its production feed may arrive late. A stakeholder asks for exports and trade booking too. Write a goal, one vertical slice, three acceptance criteria, a risk response and one retrospective measure.

Compare your plan

A reasonable goal is to validate read-only RFQ visibility for one instrument. Use labelled fixtures to build and test the contract, while keeping production integration explicitly incomplete. Defer exports and booking. Check authorization, duplicate IDs and missing values. Name the upstream dependency owner and a decision date. Demonstrate a fixture-backed preview without claiming it is live. Measure review waiting time and escaped defects. Other plans can work if they keep the outcome useful, expose the dependency and preserve quality.

Rehearse aloud

How do you handle a mid-Sprint priority change?

Explain the impact on the goal, capacity and quality. Discuss a scope trade with the Product Owner and Developers. Record the revised plan and communicate displaced work. Use a real example only if you have one.

A team finishes more tickets but customers see no improvement. What do you inspect?

Check whether tickets deliver a usable outcome. Look at release delays, defects, user feedback and work split into disconnected layers. Throughput measures flow; it does not prove customer value.

What is the difference between a Review and a retrospective?

The Review inspects the product result and future direction with stakeholders. The retrospective improves how the team works. Give a product feedback example and a separate process improvement example.

MCQ: A feature passes acceptance checks but fails the team's required security tests. A: Mark it done. B: Keep it unfinished.

B. The item has not met the Definition of Done. Report its actual state and resolve the failure rather than weakening the quality requirement to meet a date.
