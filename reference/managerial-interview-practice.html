Managerial and hiring-manager interview practice

A managerial round may be a discussion with your future manager, not an interview to become a manager. Confirm which applies. For a senior individual contributor, use evidence of technical ownership and influence. For a people-manager role, also discuss delegation, development, hiring, and performance support. State plainly if you have never had direct reports.

How to practise

Choose a question. Identify a real example and your authority in that situation. Answer in two minutes, then open the checkpoints. Explain the goal, options, action, result, and lesson. Where a prompt is hypothetical, describe your proposed action rather than claiming you already did it.

1. What would you do in your first 90 days?

Start by learning users, architecture, delivery risks, and team responsibilities. Agree on one small useful improvement before promising a rewrite. Propose checkpoints with your manager and measurable outcomes after you understand the baseline.

Follow-up: an urgent incident consumes the first month. What changes? Reprioritize with the manager and preserve learning from the incident rather than silently retaining an unrealistic plan.

2. Two stakeholders both need their work this week. How do you choose?

Clarify impact, urgency, dependencies, effort, and the cost of delay. Offer sequencing or reduced scope. Name the person accountable for the priority decision and communicate what will not ship. Do not quietly promise both.

Follow-up: both sponsors are senior. How do you make the conflict visible without escalating a personal dispute?

3. Delivery is slipping. When do you tell your manager?

Give an early warning when evidence changes the forecast. Separate completed work, uncertainty, blockers, and remaining scope. Offer options with consequences and an agreed next update. Explain an actual example if you have one.

Follow-up: your original estimate was wrong. Own that error and explain the new evidence without blaming the team.

4. Product wants to ship an AI action that can change customer data.

Clarify the value and the irreversible risk. Propose a read-only or approval-gated first release, scoped permissions, audit evidence, evaluations, and a stop mechanism. Agree on release criteria and ownership. A warning in the prompt is not sufficient authorization control.

Follow-up: leadership accepts the commercial risk. Which controls still need a clear accountable decision?

5. How do you balance technical debt and features?

Describe concrete effects such as incident risk or slower changes, not a dislike of old code. Compare a small repair, incremental replacement, and no change. Tie work to a delivery plan and check whether it helped. Avoid promising that every cleanup pays for itself.

Follow-up: the proposed cleanup has no measurable benefit yet. What small investigation would justify it?

6. How do you delegate a task you could finish faster yourself?

Agree on the outcome, constraints, decision authority, and review points. Leave implementation choices with the owner where safe. Offer context and feedback without taking back every hard decision. Separate mentoring a peer from managing their performance.

Follow-up: the first attempt is weak. Discuss the specific gap, help them revise, and adjust support before assuming they cannot do it.

7. Someone is repeatedly missing expectations. What do you do?

For a peer, discuss the delivery interface and offer help; involve the actual manager for performance matters. As their manager, clarify expectations with specific examples, listen for blockers, agree on support and follow-up, and follow company policy. Keep the discussion private and avoid diagnosing personal motives.

Follow-up: expectations were never clear. Correct that before describing the situation as an individual's failure.

8. Your strongest engineer is a bottleneck.

Identify concentrated knowledge, approval queues, and workload. Use documentation, pairing, delegated decisions, and backup ownership. Recognize their contribution without rewarding dependence on them.

Follow-up: they resist sharing ownership. Ask what risk concerns them and design a safe handoff with evidence.

9. How do you handle disagreement with your manager?

Explain the concern, evidence, alternatives, and consequence. Listen for context you lack. Once a legitimate decision is made, communicate and execute it professionally. For serious safety or ethical concerns, use appropriate escalation channels rather than concealing the risk.

Follow-up: what would make you change your position?

10. A client changes scope halfway through an FDE project.

Restate the outcome and separate new requirements from defects in the agreed work. Show impact on time, dependencies, access, and acceptance tests. Offer a revised increment and confirm the agreement. Do not expose another client's information to justify your choice.

Follow-up: the salesperson already promised it. Resolve the commitment with the accountable stakeholders, not through a surprise refusal at delivery.

11. How would you hire and onboard an engineer?

Start with the work and competencies required. Use consistent, job-relevant evidence and allow candidates to clarify assumptions. Record observations rather than personality impressions. Plan access, a buddy, a bounded first task, and feedback points for onboarding.

Follow-up: distinguish decisions you actually owned from interviews you merely participated in.

12. How do you judge team success?

Use delivery outcomes, quality, operational burden, and the team's ability to work sustainably. Explain how you avoid mistaking activity counts for value. Ask for context before comparing teams. Include customer feedback and limitations in the evidence.

Follow-up: delivery speed improves while incidents rise. Investigate the trade-off instead of reporting only the favorable metric.

13. Tell me about developing another engineer.

Identify their goal, support you provided, feedback, and evidence of greater independence. Credit their work and avoid private performance details. Explain a time you stepped back so they could own a decision.

Follow-up: what did you change when your original coaching approach did not help?

14. Why lead technically rather than become a people manager?

State the work you want to do and the accountability you are willing to own. Neither path is automatically more senior. Use examples of the role you enjoy without claiming management experience you do not have.

Follow-up: how much coding do you expect, and does that match this vacancy?

A worked answer outline

This is a hypothetical planning example, not a story to claim as your own.

“Two teams requested changes for the same release. I would first check the customer impact and dependencies with each owner. If capacity could support only one, I would present that constraint with a smaller alternative for the other. The accountable product owner would decide the order. I would record the excluded scope and review date, then update both teams. I would check whether the first release achieved its intended outcome before taking on the deferred work.”

This explains a process, but gives no evidence of past delivery. For a past-experience question, replace it with a real event, your actual decision authority, and the observed result. If you have no equivalent experience, say so.

Questions for your future manager

  • What decisions can I make independently?
  • What does success look like at three and six months?
  • How do you give feedback when someone is missing expectations?
  • Which commitments compete for this team's time?
  • What is the balance of coding, coordination, and people management?
  • How are incidents, on-call, and after-hours recovery handled?

Run a 30-minute rehearsal

Spend five minutes on motivation and role scope, fifteen on three questions with follow-ups, five on your questions, and five on feedback. Check whether each answer contains an owner, a decision, evidence, and a limitation. Rehearse the weakest answer tomorrow. Ask your teacher or a trusted peer to challenge a missing assumption.

These are original practice scenarios. For one employer's expectations of senior technical leadership, consult Amazon's senior engineer guide. It does not define every company's managerial round.
