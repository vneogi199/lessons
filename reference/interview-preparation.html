Senior interview preparation: start to offer

Use this path for senior full-stack, AI engineering, and technical-lead roles. It covers technical work, project evidence, behavioural questions, culture, hiring-manager discussions, and compensation. Interview formats differ. Ask your recruiter which rounds, assessment tools, and role expectations apply before choosing what to practise.

Prepare in this order

  1. Clarify the role. Save the job description. Ask about seniority, hands-on versus management duties, coding language, system-design format, take-home time limit, and whether AI tools are allowed. Clarify location, time zones, and on-call expectations.
  2. Map your evidence. For each requirement, write a real example, a learning project, or “not yet demonstrated.” Review every CV claim. Do not present a prototype as production experience.
  3. Prepare your introduction and project. Write a 90-second introduction and a five-minute project walkthrough. Name your responsibility, one decision, one failure, and evidence of the result.
  4. Prepare six to eight stories. Cover ownership, conflict, failure, ambiguity, mentoring, feedback, delivery pressure, and integrity. Use the behavioural worksheet linked below.
  5. Practise technical rounds. Solve a task aloud, test it, then defend a changed requirement. Use the relevant lessons to repair gaps instead of rereading the whole curriculum.
  6. Rehearse people and offer discussions. Use the manager, culture, and salary guides. Prepare questions that help you decide whether you want the job.
  7. Run a mock and repeat weak answers. Ask a peer or your teacher to interrupt with follow-ups. Revisit the weakest answer the next day without notes.

For 6–8 hours a week, a suggested practice cycle is two hours of coding and SQL, two hours of design and project explanation, one hour of behavioural or manager questions, and one hour of mock feedback. Use any extra time for the weakest area. Repeat the cycle; it is not a promise of readiness within a fixed number of weeks.

Choose a round

Recruiter screen and introduction

Prepare “Tell me about yourself,” “Why this role?”, “Why move now?”, availability, and expectations. Keep your reason for moving factual and forward-looking. Do not disclose confidential details about your current employer.

Introduction outline: current engineering scope → relevant project and personal contribution → what you want to own next. Explain employment gaps or a change of direction briefly and truthfully; share only the personal detail you are comfortable sharing.

Ask: What will this person own? What distinguishes this level from the one below? What are the interview stages and decision timeline? Are there materials or accommodations I should arrange?

Python, SQL, API coding, and take-home assignments

Start with Python, SQL and data systems, and RFQ API practice. Use RFQ questions to check terminology, not as a substitute for writing code.

Practice task: receive RFQ updates, validate required fields, retain the latest valid update per ID, join instrument data, and return a stable sorted result. Agree on duplicate timestamps, missing references, and time zones first. Add tests for invalid input, an empty batch, duplicates, and out-of-order updates. In SQL, explain join cardinality and latest-row tie-breaking.

For take-homes, record assumptions, a short run command, tests, known limits, and what you would change for production. Follow the employer's AI-use policy. Review every generated diff and disclose assistance when requested. Do not deploy or spend money unless the assignment authorizes it.

System design, AI depth, and production judgment

Use track design cases and production AI questions. Clarify users, workload, data sensitivity, consistency, and success criteria before drawing components.

Walk through one successful request and one failed request. Explain schema, API contracts, retries, idempotency, permissions, observability, and rollout. For AI work, include a non-AI baseline, retrieval evidence, evaluation cases, action approval, and cost and latency budgets. If you lack a measurement, label it an assumption and explain how you would measure it.

Project deep dive

Use the project guide for the RFQ blotter and other real projects. Prepare a diagram you can redraw, your ownership boundary, an alternative you rejected, and a limitation. Distinguish team results from your personal actions. Never invent clients, scale, savings, or incident ownership.

Behavioural interview

Use the story worksheet and 24 questions with answer checkpoints. Keep the situation brief. Explain what you did, why, what changed, and what you learned. Rehearse both a short answer and a deeper follow-up.

Culture and working-style discussion

Use the two-way culture questions. Discuss feedback, disagreement, inclusion, remote collaboration, and sustainable delivery. You are evaluating working practices, not trying to match someone's personality. Ask for a recent example when an answer sounds like a slogan.

Hiring-manager and managerial interview

Use the managerial question bank and mock. Prepare ownership, prioritization, stakeholder communication, delegation, and technical leadership. The guide separates a senior individual-contributor interview from a people-manager interview.

Salary, offer, and closing discussion

Use the salary scripts and offer worksheet. Practise asking for the range, responding to an offer, requesting time, negotiating priorities, and declining professionally. Compare recurring fixed pay, variable pay, equity, benefits, and conditions separately. Do not invent another offer or use an unverified market number.

Before accepting, clarify the role, level, reporting line, location, start date, package, conditions, and decision deadline in writing. Keep legal and tax questions for a qualified local adviser.

Run a mock loop

Split these sessions across days if needed. These times are rehearsal choices, not a prediction of an employer's schedule.

  1. 45 minutes: coding or SQL, including tests and one changed input.
  2. 45 minutes: system design, including failure handling and one changed constraint.
  3. 30 minutes: project explanation and ownership follow-ups.
  4. 40 minutes: behavioural, culture, and managerial questions.
  5. 15 minutes: recruiter and salary role-play using placeholder figures.

For each answer, score clarity, correctness where applicable, ownership, evidence, and trade-off reasoning from 0 to 2: absent, vague, specific. This is a practice rubric, not a hiring score. Write the weakest point and repeat that answer tomorrow. Ask a peer to check whether your explanation makes sense without private project context.

Before the actual interview, check the meeting link, audio, permitted editor, and required documents. Ask permission before recording. Afterward, write down topics to practise without copying confidential interview materials. Ask the recruiter about the next step and send a brief, specific thank-you if appropriate.

Need help? Give your teacher the question and your attempted answer. Ask for one challenging follow-up and feedback on the evidence, rather than a polished fictional story.
