Find the problem before building the agent

Study one section per sitting. Read its explanation, try the question, then open the answer. The linked pack carries one fictional maintenance workflow from discovery through acceptance and handover. Use it to practise senior FDE interviews. Do not present its numbers as your experience.

Ask about work that actually happened

A user asks for a chatbot. Yesterday, a repair request waited three hours because nobody knew which team owned it. A chatbot may not fix that delay. First watch one request move through the current process.

The Mom Test approach helps avoid questions that invite praise for your idea. Ask about past behavior and specific difficulties. “Would an AI dispatcher help?” produces a weaker signal than “Show me the last delayed repair.” See Rob Fitzpatrick's book.

Use 5 Whys to investigate a chain of causes, not to force exactly five answers or blame a person. “Dispatch was late” → “the owner was unknown” → “two teams use different equipment IDs.” Validate each link with records. Multiple causes may coexist.

A persona summarizes a relevant user role and its constraints. A job-to-be-done describes the progress that person needs in a situation: “During an outage, find an available qualified technician without losing the service deadline.” A buyer may care about cost; the dispatcher may care about safe assignments. Interview both.

  1. Request approved SOPs, a role map, redacted examples, baseline metrics, a data dictionary and access rules. Agree on recording consent and permitted storage.
  2. Run a 45-minute workshop: ten minutes on goals, twenty on a recent case, ten on exceptions, five on decisions and owners. Invite an end user, process owner and IT representative.
  3. Ask: What starts the work? Who receives it? What data is missing? Where does it wait? What goes wrong? Who fixes it? What is the volume? How is success measured? What must never be automated? Who accepts the result?
  4. Separate observed facts, assumptions and open questions. Confirm the written problem statement with the user. Add new requests to a change log rather than silently expanding scope.
Try: the sponsor says “staff waste four hours every day.” Can that become the ROI baseline?

Not yet. Identify the population, sample period, task boundaries and evidence. Separate hands-on effort from waiting. Observe representative users, including exceptions, before estimating avoidable labor.

Draw ownership and waiting

AS-IS means the current process. TO-BE means the proposed process. Draw both before selecting automation. A swim-lane shows who performs each step. In BPMN, pools represent participants, lanes divide responsibilities, tasks represent work, events represent occurrences, and gateways control branching. Sequence flows stay within a pool; message flows connect separate participants. See the BPMN specification.

Illustrative lane map, not executable BPMN:
Reporter:     report fault ─────────────────────────────── receive update
Dispatcher:        validate → find equipment → check skill → assign
Technician:                                                   inspect → report
External ERP pool:     ← equipment request / response →      ← status message
Alternative: missing equipment → manual lookup → validate again

Mark handling time and queue time separately. Add a timeout and failed-response path. For a formal BPMN drawing, use an exclusive gateway for mutually exclusive alternatives; a parallel gateway starts or joins concurrent paths. Do not use a parallel join for branches where only one can run.

Automate an authorized read or a repeatable validation first. Keep dispatch approval where safety or policy requires it. Highlight proposed changes with text as well as color so the diagram remains accessible.

Try: which map exposes a three-hour approval delay?

One that records arrival and departure at the approval step, its owner and the escalation path. A diagram with only tasks hides the wait. Faster model inference does not remove that queue.

Connect BRD, TDD and tests

The BRD explains the business problem, scope and required outcomes. The Technical Design Document explains implementation behavior. Here TDD means a document, not test-driven development. Teams may combine HLD and LLD in an SDD; agree on names before reviewing.

Start with the ten numbered requirements in the worked pack. Give each requirement an acceptance test, design rule and owner. Record document version, approver and changes. If an SLA changes, update the rule, test and monitoring threshold together.

Include an executive summary, scope and exclusions, current/future processes, functional requirements, integrations, data needs, acceptance criteria, risks and assumptions. “Fast” is not testable. Specify the workload, measurement boundary, percentile, window and target.

Try: a stakeholder adds automatic purchases after signing off read-only retrieval.

Record a change request. Assess authorization, financial exposure, vendor integration, testing and schedule. Obtain a revised decision. Do not treat a new irreversible action as a small prompt edit.

Make decisions easy to inspect

Map users, process owners, IT, security and sponsors by interest and decision authority. A sponsor's approval does not grant database access. Record who can decide scope, accept risk and authorize release.

Send a weekly update with the outcome achieved, evidence, next step, risk and decision needed. Meeting minutes need decisions, owners and dates. For an executive demo, use three slides: measured problem, proposed intervention, results and limitations. Show the operator's task before architecture.

When languages differ, use an agreed glossary and confirm the requirement in the user's preferred language. Arrange an authorized interpreter when needed. Do not send confidential transcripts to an unapproved translator. Saying “I need to verify that” is safer than pretending certainty.

Try: “Can we promise Friday?”

State what is ready, what depends on another team, and the safe scope for Friday. Offer options with consequences. Record the decision instead of making an unconditional promise.

UAT, release and hypercare

QA checks technical behavior. UAT asks representative users whether the system supports agreed business work. Both matter. Use the pack's fifteen scenarios with preconditions, steps, expected result, actual result and evidence.

Enter UAT with a versioned build, approved test data, working access, trained participants and known defects. Exit only when agreed scenarios pass and the named owners accept remaining risks. Severity describes impact; priority describes how soon to act. An authorization leak blocks this example's release even if it is rarely triggered.

Before go-live, reconcile migrated data, rehearse rollback, verify support contacts and check dashboards. Rolling back code does not undo ERP writes. Reconcile external effects separately. During hypercare, staff a named support rota, review failures daily and use explicit escalation thresholds. End hypercare when the agreed stability and support-readiness criteria hold, not merely when the calendar runs out.

Try: all unit tests pass, but dispatchers cannot recover a rejected assignment.

UAT has found a business workflow failure. Add the recovery path and regression checks. Unit-test success does not establish operational acceptance.

Help people use the system without you

Prosci's ADKAR model distinguishes Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability and Reinforcement. Apply it to one role: explain the reason for change, understand concerns, teach the task, observe practice, then support continued use. See the model owner's explanation.

A dispatcher may understand the tool yet avoid it because its errors create extra work. More training alone will not fix that. Observe the task and address the failure. Supply role-based guides, a short captioned walkthrough, an FAQ and a human escalation route. Include accessibility and language needs.

Measure first-time fix rate, SLA compliance, repair duration and adoption alongside errors. Define denominators and exclusions before reporting. Separate elapsed-time improvement from cash savings. The worked pack includes synthetic arithmetic and a handover checklist.

Interview: usage is high but complaints increase. What next?

Inspect successful task completion, rework and mandatory usage. Segment by role and case type. High login counts may reflect confusion. Use interviews and trace evidence to choose one improvement and measure it again.
