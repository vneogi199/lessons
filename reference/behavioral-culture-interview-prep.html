Senior behavioral and culture interview preparation

Continue with salary negotiation scripts and offer comparison and project walkthroughs, including your RFQ blotter.

Use this with lessons 0628 and 0634–0644. Prepare evidence of how you make decisions, work with people and respond when plans fail. Culture fit is a two-way discussion about working practices and values. You do not need to imitate an interviewer's personality or agree with every preference.

These are original rehearsal prompts, not questions attributed to a specific employer. Answer from real experience. Keep client names, financial positions, private metrics and colleague identities confidential. If you lack a matching example, say so and explain a related experience or label your answer hypothetical.

Build a small story bank

Prepare six to eight stories that cover delivery, disagreement, failure, ambiguity, mentoring and integrity. One story can demonstrate several skills, but avoid forcing every question into the same project. Your RFQ blotter work may offer examples of upstream dependencies, trader feedback and downstream booking boundaries. Include only responsibilities and outcomes you actually owned.

Story title:
Situation: What was happening? Who was affected?
Task: What were you responsible for? What was outside your authority?
Decision: Which options did you consider? What constraint mattered?
Actions: What did you personally do, and how did others contribute?
Result: What changed? What evidence can you safely share?
Reflection: What did you learn? What would you change now?
Follow-up: What happened after the immediate result?

STAR helps organize the answer: situation, task, action and result. Add the decision and reflection when they explain your judgment. Keep the setup brief and spend most of the answer on actions. Use “I” for your contribution and “we” for shared work. If no reliable metric exists, give an observable outcome such as a completed rollout, a resolved support issue or a documented decision. Do not fabricate a percentage.

Practise a 30-second summary and a two-minute version. A longer follow-up can explain technical details. The listener should be able to identify your role without guessing.

Questions with answer checkpoints

Answer before opening the checkpoints. They describe evidence to include, not a script to memorize.

1. Tell me about yourself.

Connect your current engineering scope to two relevant experiences and the role you want next. Explain what you have built and the decisions you owned. Keep the career chronology short. Separate professional AI work from recent learning projects.

Follow-up: what can you contribute immediately, and where would you need support?

2. Why this role and this company?

Choose a concrete problem or responsibility from the role. Connect it to your experience and the work you want to do. Use company information you actually checked. Name an unanswered question instead of inventing enthusiasm for an unfamiliar product.

Follow-up: would the role still interest you if the first six months focused on integration and maintenance?

3. Describe a project you owned from an unclear request to delivery.

Explain how you clarified users, success criteria and boundaries. Describe the first useful release, the risks you tracked and how you sought feedback. Identify what you delegated and what you personally decided. State the result and remaining limitations.

Follow-up: what did you deliberately leave out, and who agreed to that scope?

4. Tell me about a serious disagreement with another engineer.

State both proposals fairly. Explain the constraint behind the disagreement, the evidence you gathered and how the decision was made. Describe your conduct after the decision. Avoid making your colleague a villain.

Follow-up: what evidence would have changed your own position?

5. When did you influence a team without formal authority?

Identify who owned the decision and what they cared about. Explain how you listened, tested a proposal or reduced the cost of trying it. Distinguish persuasion from bypassing ownership. Describe the adoption or rejection honestly.

Follow-up: what happened when an important stakeholder still disagreed?

6. Tell me about a mistake you made.

Choose a real mistake with meaningful consequences that you can discuss safely. Own your action, explain detection and mitigation, and describe a concrete change afterward. Do not disguise a strength as a failure or claim sole blame for a systemic issue.

Follow-up: what evidence shows that the follow-up reduced the risk?

7. Describe an incident you helped resolve.

Separate symptom, impact, hypothesis and confirmed cause. Explain your role during mitigation, who communicated with affected users and how recovery was verified. Describe prevention or detection improvements. Do not invent production experience from a lab.

Follow-up: which early hypothesis was wrong, and how did you reject it?

8. How did you handle a deadline that looked impossible?

Describe the estimate, dependencies and risks you exposed. Explain options such as reducing scope, sequencing delivery or moving the date. State who accepted the trade-off. Avoid presenting repeated hidden overtime as the only solution.

Follow-up: what would you do if the stakeholder refused every proposed adjustment?

9. Tell me about a time you challenged a product requirement.

Explain the user need before criticizing the requested implementation. Show how you gathered evidence and proposed an alternative. Include the effect on usability, reliability or delivery. Describe how you handled a decision to keep the original requirement.

Follow-up: what assumption about the user did you initially get wrong?

10. When did you choose a simpler technical solution?

Describe the expected workload, team constraints and cost of the more complex option. Explain the limits of your choice and what signal would justify replacing it. Avoid arguing that simplicity always wins.

Follow-up: did the simplifying assumption remain true after launch?

11. Describe useful feedback that was difficult to hear.

State the behavior the feedback concerned and your initial response. Explain what you changed, how you checked progress and whether you sought another review. You can disagree with part of the feedback while still learning from it.

Follow-up: what would your colleague say changed?

12. Tell me about someone you mentored.

Describe the person's goal without exposing private performance information. Explain how you adjusted support, gave actionable feedback and allowed them to own decisions. Credit their work. Use evidence of increased independence rather than claiming their success as yours.

Follow-up: when did you step back instead of solving the problem for them?

13. How do you review code when delivery is urgent?

Explain how you distinguish correctness, security and maintainability risks from preferences. Describe one concrete review and the tests or evidence you requested. Agree on explicit follow-up for non-blocking issues instead of silently dropping them.

Follow-up: the author is more senior and dismisses your concern. How do you make the risk clear and escalate proportionately?

14. Tell me about work across teams or time zones.

Describe handoffs, written decisions and ownership boundaries. Explain how you handled a misunderstanding without blaming nationality or communication style. State which information needed synchronous discussion and which could be documented.

Follow-up: how did you avoid making one location carry all inconvenient meeting times?

15. Describe a decision made with incomplete information.

Separate facts from assumptions. Explain what you could learn cheaply, whether the decision was reversible and how you limited downside. State the review trigger and what happened when more information arrived.

Follow-up: which uncertainty was too important to accept without investigation?

16. Tell me about a time you changed your mind.

Give your original reasoning, the new evidence and the revised decision. Explain how you communicated the change and handled work already done. Changing your mind is useful only when the reason is clear.

Follow-up: how do you avoid repeatedly changing direction without sufficient evidence?

17. How do you handle a struggling dependency team?

Explain the delivery impact, the team's constraints and the options you discussed together. Consider a smaller contract, an adapter or a staged rollout. Keep commitments and escalation visible. Avoid quietly absorbing unlimited work or assigning blame without evidence.

Follow-up: what if your own team caused part of their delay?

18. Tell me about balancing technical debt and feature delivery.

Choose a specific debt item and explain its observed cost or risk. Compare a targeted repair with postponement or a broad rewrite. State how you measured progress and who accepted the remaining risk.

Follow-up: what would make you stop the cleanup and return to feature work?

19. How would you respond to pressure to ship an AI feature without evaluation?

Clarify the proposed capability and potential harm. Suggest a bounded release, human review or reduced scope with explicit tests. State which unsafe actions must remain disabled. Communicate the unresolved risks and decision owner. Label this hypothetical unless you have a real example.

Follow-up: what minimum evidence would permit a read-only pilot but still block autonomous actions?

20. Tell me about protecting a user's or client's trust.

Describe the conflict, your responsibility and how you made the risk visible. Explain the safer alternative and any escalation. Do not reveal the sensitive information in the interview itself. Use a hypothetical answer if you lack an appropriate real story.

Follow-up: what if the risky shortcut had a large commercial benefit?

21. What working environment helps you do your best work?

Describe practical conditions such as clear ownership, useful feedback or time for focused work. Explain how you adapt when those conditions are imperfect. Give an example instead of a list of personality labels.

Follow-up: which preference is flexible, and which boundary matters to you?

22. How do you respond when your preferred proposal loses?

Explain how you check that risks and ownership are understood, document the decision and support implementation. Revisit it when agreed evidence changes. Distinguish ordinary disagreement from a safety or integrity concern that requires further escalation.

Follow-up: when is “disagree and commit” an inappropriate response?

23. What would you do in your first 90 days?

Begin with users, system boundaries and team expectations. Propose a small deliverable and learn the release and incident processes. Discuss a plan with your manager rather than promising a rewrite before seeing the system.

Follow-up: you inherit an urgent production incident in week one. How would you contribute without acting beyond your knowledge or access?

24. Why are you leaving, and what do you want to change?

Give a truthful, professional explanation focused on scope, growth or working conditions you seek. Avoid confidential disputes and personal attacks. Connect your reason to questions you will ask about the new role so you do not repeat the same mismatch.

Follow-up: what evidence would make you decline this opportunity?

Assess the company as well

Ask for recent examples. A slogan is less informative than how the team handled a real decision. Compare answers across interviewers without treating one awkward answer as proof of a bad culture.

  • What happened the last time someone challenged a senior person's design?
  • How does the team decide what to cut when a deadline becomes unrealistic?
  • Who owns production incidents, and how is after-hours work shared and recovered?
  • What changed after the last post-incident review?
  • How are engineers evaluated beyond ticket count and visible delivery?
  • How do product and engineering resolve disagreements about risk?
  • What can an engineer stop or escalate when an AI feature behaves unsafely?
  • How does the team support different time zones and communication styles?
  • What would successful performance in this role look like after six months?
  • Why is the role open, and which part of the work is hardest to retain ownership of?

Look for specific ownership, examples of feedback changing decisions and realistic constraints. Investigate contradictory expectations, blame-heavy stories or unclear incident duties. Ask a follow-up before drawing a conclusion. Assess whether you can work effectively under the actual expectations, not whether you share hobbies with the team.

A 40-minute rehearsal

  1. Five minutes: introduction and motivation.
  2. Twenty minutes: four stories, with one challenging follow-up each.
  3. Five minutes: a hypothetical AI delivery or integrity scenario.
  4. Five minutes: your questions about working practices.
  5. Five minutes: feedback and one answer to repeat tomorrow.

Score each answer from 0 to 2 on clarity, personal contribution, decision reasoning, evidence and reflection. Zero means absent, one means vague, and two means specific. Use the score to choose practice, not predict a hiring result. Note whether you answered the actual question, fairly represented other people and handled the changed constraint.

Do not write a learning record claiming mastery merely because you read the answers. Rehearse with a peer or your teacher. If recording, get the other person's permission and keep private work details out of the recording.
